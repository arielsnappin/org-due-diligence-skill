# What good looks like

What separates a useful dossier from a pile of facts is judgment on every line. These are the moves that made this skill's best early outputs genuinely decision-changing. Use them in every Org Dossier and Decision Brief.

Worked examples: `examples/org-dossier-example.md` and `examples/decision-brief-example.md`. They are fictional. Match their depth, voice, and moves, but never reuse their facts or force their structure onto a different situation.

## The length rule: dense, not long

A dossier runs 1–2 pages and a Decision Brief 2–3. That's the length of a sharp memo from a trusted advisor. It is not a 50-page report, and it is not a skeleton of bullets either.

- Every sentence should carry a fact, a judgment, or an action.
- Cut filler, never nuance.
- Tables organize facts and prose carries judgment, so follow every table with what it tells you.
- In chat, lead with the bottom line and the few things that matter most. The full document holds the rest.

## The moves

**1. Answer their question, not a generic one.** Lead with the bottom line for *their* goal, and name the catch.
> "The catch for your question: Company A gives you the Head of Product job before the title. The offer says Director; 'grows into Head of Product' is the recruiter's phrase, not the offer letter's."

**2. Compare everything to the alternatives.** A fact without a comparison is trivia.
> "Company B's VP has been in seat three years and promoted two PMs this year. At Company A you'd report to the founder, with no product leader above you to learn from."

**3. After every table, say what it tells you.** Give two or three observations, each with a "so what".
> "Two observations. Company A posts no Head of Product band, so the 'grows into it' path hasn't been designed on paper yet. And Company A's Director band sits below Company B's, so the bigger title doesn't come with bigger base pay."

**4. Connect dots into patterns.** People, dates, and events add up to one insight.
> "The last Head of Product lasted 11 months and left for a lateral role. The founder says they still read every spec. Either fact alone is noise. Together, they're the whole risk."

**5. Treat gaps as signals.** What's missing is often the story.
> "Design has no named leader, and nobody has said whether design would report to you. That's either scope you'd gain or a turf question you'd inherit. Ask which."

**6. Tie it to their profile.** Say how the role maps to what they've actually done and what they want.
> "Your analytics product line is the closest match to their core product on any board you're looking at. You'd be credible with their customers on day one."

**7. Reframe honestly.** If the question they asked isn't the real decision, say so, plainly and kindly.
> "None of these puts you in a Head of Product seat through a normal promotion. The real question isn't which company. It's whether you'd rather bet on a founder or on a ladder."

**8. Caveat, then ask.** Don't just hedge. Turn the uncertainty into a question.
> "These are base salaries only. Company A's equity is the swing factor, and its value can't be known from outside. Ask for the last 409A, the preferred price, and the fully diluted share count."

**9. Show timing and process implications.**
> "Company A's last step is Thursday's founder call, and Company B's offer holds for two weeks. That's enough time, as long as Company A's process doesn't slip."

**10. Make asks tactical and time-aware.** Say what to ask, whom to ask, when, and what waiting costs.
> "Ask Company B's VP what it would take for the next Director seat to go to someone internal. Ask now, while it's a curious question rather than a negotiating one."

**11. Coach the delicate conversations.** Give the sequence, the exact words, what a real answer sounds like, and the caution.
> "Don't ask the founder whether they'll let go, because everyone says yes. Ask for evidence: 'Which product decisions did you make last month that you'd want me to make three months from now?' A real answer names decisions and a handover. 'We'll figure it out together' is a non-answer."

**12. Correct yourself in the open.** If a new fact overturns an earlier claim, say so at the top of the next version and show the corrected picture.
> "Correction: v1 compared LinkedIn's 'associated members', which for Company B includes about 3,000 implementation partners. The company says it has about 1,200 employees, and v2 uses that number."

**13. End with what you'd actually do.** Make it concrete, sequenced, and dated, and include the one sharp question for each conversation.
> "Tuesday: ask your mutual connection for an intro to the previous Head of Product. It's the most informative 15 minutes you can spend this week."

**14. State source reliability up front.** Add one line under the title covering what the sources are and which ones lag or are crowd-maintained.
> "Titles are self-reported and can lag. The org chart is crowd-maintained, so treat reporting lines as directional."

**15. Sound like a trusted senior mentor.** Be direct, specific, and warm, and validate good instincts ("Your instinct that the founder is the real question is right"). Never flatter, never alarm.

## Anti-patterns

- Facts with no "so what".
- Tables with no interpretation.
- Hedging with no question attached ("it's hard to say").
- Generic advice that would fit any company ("culture matters").
- Numbers without the base, the date, or a note on what they measure.
- A recommendation that ignores the user's own goals.
- A one-page summary standing in for the reasoning. The visual cover summarizes the memo; it doesn't replace it.
