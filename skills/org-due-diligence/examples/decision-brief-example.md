# Company A vs. Company B vs. staying: a founder or a ladder?
*Illustrative example: every company, person, and number here is fictional. v1 · Sep 29, 2026 · ★ = matters most to you (path to the next level; scope & team) · Companion docs: dossier-company-a.md, dossier-company-b.md · Visual cover: cover.html*

## Bottom line

I'm leaning toward **Company A, with conditions.** It's the only option that puts you in the top product seat within two years. But that job is only real if the founder hands over product, and the last person in the seat lasted 11 months. You can find out which it is before Thursday's call ends.

## The thing that reframes your question

You asked which option gets you to Head of Product within two years. Honestly, none of them does it through a normal promotion.

- **Company A** hands you the job now: first product leader, exec seat, reporting to the founder. It doesn't come with the title, and not necessarily with the authority.
- **Company B** has the best manager and the best platform in the set, but its last two Director seats went to outside hires. The ladder you'd be climbing ends two rungs below where you want to be.
- **Staying** is safe, and your manager is in your corner. But product headcount is down about 10% this year and two leaders have left, and shrinking orgs don't create seats.

So the real question isn't "which company". It's **whether you'd rather bet on a founder or on a ladder.** A founder bet pays off fast or fails fast, and you can test it this week. A ladder bet is slower and safer, but at Company B the ladder has a known gap at exactly the rung you need.

## Option by option

**Company A.**
- **What it buys:** the top product job on day one, an exec-team seat, and a product that directly matches your analytics background.
- **What it costs:** a manager who is also the product's author, a team with a likely internal candidate, and a seat that has already turned over once.
- **What has to be true:** the founder hands over real decisions, the previous Head of Product's story isn't "they never let me lead", and the office policy clears your must-have. You can know all three within a week.

**Company B.**
- **What it buys:** the strongest manager in the set, a real platform with 30 engineers, the best-paid and most liquid package, and a growing company.
- **What it costs:** time. The VP promoted two PMs this year, both to Group PM rather than Director, and Director seats have gone to outsiders twice in 18 months. It's a great next job and a slow path to the one after.
- **On headcount:** the company says about 1,200 employees. LinkedIn's ~4,800 "associated members" includes roughly 3,000 implementation partners, so ignore that number.

**Staying.**
- **What it buys:** a manager in your corner, known rhythms, and no risk this year.
- **What it costs:** the org is shrinking, and the seat you want isn't open.
- **If you stay,** do it on purpose rather than by default: have the path conversation in March and ask for a date.

## On money

Base pay won't decide this.
- **Base:** Company A's Director band ($240–280K) sits below Company B's Director band and overlaps its Group PM band ($225–260K). Your Company B offer is at the top of that Group PM band.
- **Equity is the difference.** Company B's RSUs have had two tenders in two years, which makes it the most bankable package here. Company A's options grant is larger on paper, but its value can't be known from outside.
- **Ask Company A** for the last 409A, the preferred price, and the fully diluted share count. Don't value the grant yourself, and don't let a large share count stand in for a large number.

## The conversations that matter

**1. The founder (Thursday).** This conversation decides it. Don't ask whether they'll let go, because everyone says yes. Ask for evidence:
> "Which product decisions did you make last month that you'd want me to make three months from now?"

- **A real answer** names specific decisions and how the handover would work.
- **A non-answer** sounds like "We'll figure it out together."
- **Then ask about your predecessor:** "What happened with the previous Head of Product, and what would you do differently?" Listen for ownership rather than blame.

**2. The previous Head of Product (via your mutual connection).** These are the most informative 15 minutes you can spend this week. Keep the ask small, and never ask for anything confidential: "What would you want to know if you were in my shoes?" The draft note is in your Backchannel List.

**3. Company B's VP (before you answer their offer).** Ask one question, while it's still curiosity rather than negotiation:
> "What would it take for the next Director seat to go to someone internal, and has that happened here?"

If the answer names a path and a timeline, Company B's biggest weakness shrinks and it becomes a real contender.

## What could change the answer

1. **Will the founder hand over product decisions?** If yes, Company A is the clear pick. If they hedge, A's path and scope ratings drop and B leads. *Ask:* the founder on Thursday, and the previous Head of Product on Monday.
2. **Company A's office policy (a must-have).** More than three office days and A is out. *Ask:* the recruiter, by email, this week.
3. **Does Company B have an internal Director path?** A named path with a date closes most of the gap with A. *Ask:* the VP, before you respond to the offer.
