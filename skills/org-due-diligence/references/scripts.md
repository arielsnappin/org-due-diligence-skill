# Scripts for sensitive moments

Adapt the wording to the user's voice. Each script comes with *when* to use it and *why* it works, so the user can improvise rather than recite.

---

## Asking for more time
> "I'm genuinely excited about this, and it's a big decision. I want to make it properly so I can come in fully committed. Could I have until [date]? In the meantime it would really help to talk with [person or role] about [topic]."

**When:** as soon as the offer arrives.
**Why it works:** it frames time as commitment rather than hesitation. Pairing the ask with an engaged next step, another conversation, signals that you're serious. A company that pulled your start date forward usually has room to wait a week.

## Asking to be considered at a higher level
**Before the loop (the cheapest time to ask):**
> "Before we get started, I'd love to understand how you're leveling this role. Given [scope, team, results], I'd like to be considered at [level]. What would the loop need to show for that?"

**At the offer (costlier, so it needs evidence from the loop):**
> "I'm excited about the role. Based on the scope we discussed, [specifics], I'd like to talk about whether [level] is the right fit. What would it take?"

**Why it works:** it ties the ask to evidence and gives them a path to yes, rather than an argument to win.

## Asking about the predecessor
> "Is this a new role or a backfill? If someone held it before, what did they do well, and what would you want done differently?"

**Why it works:** the phrasing is neutral and invites a story. Listen for candor rather than a polished non-answer.

## Asking to talk with the team, or with someone who's worked for the manager
> "Before I decide, could I talk with one or two people who'd be my peers or on my team? And is there someone who's worked for you who'd be open to sharing what that's like?"

**When:** at the offer.
**Why it works:** it's a normal request at senior levels, and how they respond is a signal in itself.

## Mentioning a competing offer (to the company you're evaluating)
> "I want to be upfront: I'm in late stages elsewhere, with a decision due [date]. [Company] is [where it honestly stands for you]. Is there anything we can do to line up the timelines?"

**Why it works:** it's information, not leverage, and it gives them a fair chance to move. Never bluff. Senior hiring circles are small.

## The "stay" conversation with your current manager
Do this in two steps, and take the second one only if you'd really leave.

**Step 1: the scope conversation.** Don't mention any offer.
> "For a while now I've been operating at [scope], including [2–3 concrete facts]. I'd like to understand what the path to [title or scope] looks like here, and roughly when."

- A real answer names a cycle, specific gaps, and a date.
- A non-answer sounds like "you're doing great, let's revisit."

Either way you learn a lot, and it costs you nothing politically. It's a question good managers expect from strong people.

**Step 2: disclosure.** Only if step 1 went nowhere and you're prepared to go.
> "I want to be transparent with you: I have an offer. I'd rather grow here if there's a real path, and I wanted you to hear it from me before I decide."

**Why it works:** disclosure framed as information rather than an ultimatum lands as respect. What reads as threatening is a deadline with a number attached.
**Caution:** counteroffers often change the relationship even when the pay changes. Decide in advance what you'd actually accept. If you'd get the title but nothing else would change, would that be enough?

## Raising exits or changes you noticed (with a skip-level or founder)
> "I noticed a few leadership changes in [area] over the last year. What happened, and what did you learn from it?"

**Why it works:** it shows diligence and invites ownership. Raise only what's public, and ask with curiosity rather than as an accusation.
