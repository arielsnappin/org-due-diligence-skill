# Confidence labels and data traps

## Labels

Attach a label and a source to every factual claim that drives a rating. Everything else can be sourced in the notes at the end, which keeps the page readable.

| Label | Use when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Verified** | The source is primary (the company's site, an official posting, a filing, or the decision-maker telling the user directly about something they control), or two independent sources agree. | A pay band on the company's own careers page. The hiring manager confirming the reporting line on a call. |
| **Reported** | There's a single secondary source. | A self-reported LinkedIn title, a press article, something the recruiter said, a forward-looking promise that isn't in writing. |
| **Inferred** | It's your reasoning from patterns. Always show the reasoning. | "The removed posting and a new Director profile suggest the seat was filled." |
| **Unknown (?)** | It's a gap. It becomes a question. | Whether a Director seat is budgeted for next year. |

**Inline format:** `[Verified · careers page, Sep 2026]`, `[Reported · LinkedIn]`, `[Inferred]`. Keep it short.

## The data-trap checklist

Run this before you use any number. Each trap has fooled smart people, and trap #1 fooled an early version of this skill.

1. **Associated members ≠ employees.** LinkedIn's "associated members" count includes anyone who lists the company: contractors, partner networks, drivers, alumni with stale profiles, and people with the same company name. At marketplace companies it can be 10 or more times the real headcount. **Do:** use the company's own number, LinkedIn's self-reported size band, or counts filtered by function, and say which one you used.
2. **Tenure has the same problem.** Median tenure across a contractor-heavy population tells you nothing about the product org. **Do:** look at tenure for the specific people and function that matter.
3. **Small bases.** "+700% openings" on a base of one is noise. **Do:** always show the base ("from 1 to 8 openings").
4. **Crowd-sourced org charts lag.** Sites like TheOrg are user-maintained. **Do:** treat them as directional and never label them Verified.
5. **Titles lag and hide level.** Profiles go months without updates, and headlines like "Product @ Company" hide the level. **Do:** cross-check dates and roles, and ask about leveling directly.
6. **Postings mislead.** A removed posting isn't necessarily filled, a reposted one isn't necessarily new, one req may span several levels, and bands vary by location. **Do:** note the location of each band and don't assume a seat was filled.
7. **Valuation drifts.** The last disclosed round isn't today's value, and the preferred-share price isn't the 409A. **Do:** give the date, and turn uncertainty into questions (last 409A? a round in progress? tender history?).
8. **Review sites skew.** Glassdoor and Blind attract the unhappy and the very happy. **Do:** use them only for recurring themes, at low confidence, never as a headline.
9. **Name collisions.** Two people can share a name in the same industry. **Do:** confirm identity from company, role, and timeline before attributing anything.
10. **Staleness.** Orgs change fast. **Do:** date every source and flag anything older than 12 months.

## When sources disagree

Prefer primary over secondary sources, and recent over old. When two credible sources conflict, show both, label the claim Reported, and turn it into a question.
